How to plan insurance quote follow-up automation

A worked example and checklist for choosing a manageable first workflow—before you buy another tool.

By Neil Barris, FounderUpdated September 9, 2026

Start with a visible next action, not a longer email sequence. To plan quote follow-up, define what starts the workflow, who reviews each reminder, and what stops it. This guide gives you a worked example and a checklist you can adapt before choosing software.

In this guide
  1. Choose a manageable starting point
  2. Map the information you need
  3. Walk through a sample quote
  4. Use a simple reminder draft
  5. Test stop conditions
  6. Measure the result
  7. Check your tools and sources

Choose one follow-up gap you can describe

“Use more AI” is difficult to turn into an operating process. “Every open quote should have an owner and a next-action date” is a requirement your team can test.

Start by reviewing a small sample of recent quotes with the people who handle them. Look for a repeated problem: nobody owns the next contact, a reminder is copied into several places, or a reply does not reach the person maintaining the tracker. Record the problem without assuming automation is the answer.

A shared task view or a better use of an existing CRM feature may be enough. Consider an additional connection only when it removes a specific handoff your tools cannot already handle. If the real issue is inaccurate quote status, adding more reminders could make it worse.

Map the minimum information the workflow needs

The table below is a proposed planning model, not a specification for a particular AMS. Your platform may represent these items differently.

ItemWhy it matters
Quote referenceConnects the task to the right record without copying a policy document into every tool.
Responsible staff memberGives someone ownership of the next step and exceptions.
Sent date and current statusDistinguishes a quote actually sent from one still being prepared.
Next-action dateMakes it possible to identify overdue work.
Contact preference and stop statusPrevents the process from continuing when contact is inappropriate or no longer needed.
Last reviewed actionHelps the team avoid duplicate reminders and see what happened.

Decide which system is the source of truth. If the same status exists in a spreadsheet and an AMS, write down which one controls the workflow and how disagreements are resolved.

Walk through a sample quote from start to finish

Illustrative example: Quote Q-104 is fictional. The sequence below demonstrates a design; it is not a client result, recommended contact frequency, or live integration.

  1. Record the quote. A staff member marks Q-104 as sent and assigns an owner. The owner sets the next-action date using the agency's own process.
  2. Check when the task is due. The workflow checks that the quote is still open and is not paused or stopped. Missing information sends the task to a person instead of guessing.
  3. Prepare the next action. A task or draft reminder appears for the owner. The draft contains only the approved context it needs.
  4. Review before sending. The owner checks the record, edits the message if needed, and chooses the appropriate next step. Coverage questions are handled by staff.
  5. Close or pause the loop. A reply, a declined quote, or a do-not-contact status stops the reminder path. If a reply cannot be detected reliably, the owner must update the status manually.

The essential design question is whether the workflow knows when to stop. A sequence that keeps contacting someone after a reply creates more work and a worse client experience.

Use a reminder that does not invent policy details

This neutral draft is a starting point for staff review, not a message to send automatically to every prospect.

Subject: Following up on your quote

Hi [First name],

I’m following up on the quote we sent on [date]. Did you receive it, and is there anything you would like to discuss with [staff member]?

If your plans have changed, let us know so we can update our records.

[Staff name]
[Agency]

Replace every placeholder, verify the recipient and record, and follow your agency's communication requirements. Avoid automatically adding coverage comparisons, premium explanations, or claims about suitability.

An AI-generated variation is optional. A short approved template can be easier to review and maintain than a prompt that produces a different message each time.

Test the exceptions before using real records

Use fictional test records and an agreed acceptance checklist. At a minimum, check these scenarios:

  • A quote is open, assigned, and due: the correct owner receives one task.
  • A quote has no owner or missing contact details: it is flagged for review.
  • The prospect replies before the reminder: the reminder is stopped or presented for manual resolution.
  • The same quote is imported twice: it does not produce two reminders.
  • The record is declined, paused, or marked do not contact: the reminder path does not continue.
  • A connection fails: the team can see the failure and knows who handles it.

Also practice pausing the workflow and correcting a wrong status. The person maintaining it should be able to explain those steps without relying on the person who originally built it.

Measure staff effort and follow-up reliability separately

Before launch, count overdue follow-up tasks and open quotes with no next action. Estimate staff effort using the same method for each sample. Repeat the measurement after the team has used the new process.

Keep volume and work type comparable. A quiet week with straightforward quotes is not a fair comparison with a busy week full of exceptions. Record time spent reviewing drafts and maintaining the workflow as well as time saved on manual tracking.

For a hypothetical calculation, saving five minutes on each of 20 quotes recovers 100 minutes. That is an illustration of staff capacity, not a claim about actual results, cash savings, or sales conversion. Software fees and ongoing review can reduce the practical benefit.
